How to Select a MiniMax H3 Prompt Example by Scenario

When a prompt repository has many examples, the hard part is often not finding a prompt. It is deciding which example is a sensible place to begin. A product scene, a travel moment, a food close-up, and a character-led presentation can all look like “video ideas,” yet they ask a reader to make different editorial choices before any prompt is reused.

This guide is about that first decision: locating a scenario-relevant example in the supplied repository and choosing it as a reference for your own brief. The wording select a MiniMax H3 prompt example by scenario is a documented natural-language fallback for this reader task. It is not evidence of search demand, ranking, or volume.

The two source surfaces have different jobs. FLAQ’s MiniMax H3 Image-to-Video page is the appropriate source for information that page makes about FLAQ. The supplied MiniMax H3 prompt repository is the place to browse its examples and categories. Neither surface should be used to make the other one say more than it does.

Start with the scenario, not the most dramatic example

Before opening a category, write one short sentence about the piece you need to make. Keep it concrete enough to rule things out:

  • “A close product view that introduces one physical object.”
  • “A place-focused travel moment with a clear sense of arrival.”
  • “A food scene where preparation is the subject.”
  • “A character-led explanation for a creator audience.”

This is not a prompt template and it is not a production prescription. It is simply a selection handle. If you cannot say what the viewer is meant to notice first, it is too early to choose a repository example.

Scenario is more useful than a broad aesthetic label. “Cinematic” can describe nearly anything. “A quiet product introduction with the object as the center of attention” gives you a way to compare examples without pretending that one of them is guaranteed to fit. The goal is proximity: find an example whose subject, use case, and editorial job are near yours. You can then read it as a reference rather than as a verdict.

At the observed repository commit, the collection is arranged in numbered category files. Its current inventory is 24 category files and 84 recipe headings; that is a time-bound repository inventory, not a claim about a model or a result. Treat the category names as a map of possible starting points. They help you narrow a large shelf to a smaller one.

Turn a loose brief into a selection card

Use a small selection card before you open any candidate. It has three fields:

  1. Scenario: What kind of situation is this? For example, product, travel, food, fashion, cinematic storytelling, or an editing/localization use case.
  2. Primary subject: What needs to remain central to the idea: an object, a place, food, a person, or an abstract visual motif?
  3. Reader-facing purpose: What should the piece communicate at a glance: introduction, atmosphere, demonstration, explanation, or a transition between ideas?

The card is deliberately plain. It does not ask you to decide technical settings or a provider workflow. Those are separate questions with separate sources. For this task, the card only prevents a common mistake: choosing an example because its surface style is attractive while its underlying scenario has nothing to do with your brief.

Suppose your brief is a short product introduction. A product/e-commerce category is the first sensible place to browse because its examples are already framed around a product scenario. A travel example may still be visually appealing, but it asks different questions about place and movement. The travel example is not “wrong”; it is simply farther away from the task you wrote on the card.

If more than one category seems plausible, keep two candidates briefly rather than forcing an early choice. Then compare them with the same three fields. The candidate that requires fewer conceptual substitutions is usually the clearer reference. That is a reading decision; it does not turn the example into proof for a new brief.

Browse the repository as a map, not as a feature list

Once the card points to a category, read the category’s examples in order. First look for the one whose situation is closest. Then ask whether its subject is also close enough to be useful. A travel location and a product still life may share a color palette, but their narrative jobs differ. A useful reference gives you a way to reason about your own scenario; it does not need to donate every visual detail.

The repository also contains concepts related to different kinds of references and workflows. For EN-03, those concepts stay inside the repository context. They can help a reader understand how an example has been organized, but they do not establish what the FLAQ page offers or what any output will look like. Keeping that boundary intact makes the selection process more honest and more reusable.

Make a short note beside the candidate you choose:

  • category and recipe identifier;
  • the scenario on your selection card;
  • the shared element that made the example relevant;
  • the one element you will not carry across because it belongs only to the example’s own situation.

That final line is especially helpful. It prevents a reader from copying an entire creative premise when only the scenario structure was useful. An example about a destination can teach you how a destination is framed as a scenario without turning your product into a destination. The note keeps “reference” from quietly becoming “replication.”

Use a second candidate as a boundary check

Selection gets safer when you choose one nearby alternative. This is not a competition between prompts. It is a way to explain why the first candidate belongs to the brief.

For a product introduction, compare your chosen product example with one from a neighboring category such as fashion or food. Ask three questions: Which one puts the right subject first? Which one has the closer reader-facing purpose? Which one would require the fewest changes in scenario? If the answers point in different directions, return to the selection card and rewrite the brief more plainly.

This check also keeps EN-03 distinct from a prompt-anatomy lesson or a continuity checklist. You are not breaking down an example into a universal formula, and you are not auditing a sequence of constraints. You are deciding which repository example is the least misleading starting reference for a stated scenario.

It is fine to leave the choice unresolved when the brief is unresolved. “Two plausible references remain” is a better record than pretending one is proven best. The repository is a resource for discovery and comparison; it is not independent evidence of a provider’s performance, availability, or results.

Keep the FLAQ page and repository in their own lanes

The selection process needs only two sources. The FLAQ page is for statements made by FLAQ, and the supplied repository is for browsing its examples and categories. Keeping those jobs separate means that a repository label does not quietly become a statement about the FLAQ page.

Apply the same rule to the repository. A category label or an example can explain why it is relevant to a scenario; it cannot prove a provider feature, a request format, or a particular generated result. When you need information about one source, return to that source. When you need to select an example, return to the repository. This small separation keeps a reader from building a decision on borrowed wording.

Make a Scenario-Based MiniMax H3 Prompt-Example Selection

After you have selected a candidate, save a compact record: the category, the recipe identifier, the scenario card, and the reason for the match. Add one sentence that names the boundary: “I used this as a scenario reference, not as evidence of an outcome.” That makes the next revision easier, whether you keep the candidate or replace it later.

The most useful repository example is not necessarily the most elaborate one. It is the one that helps you articulate your own scenario without making promises the source does not make. That is the point of scenario-based MiniMax H3 prompt-example selection: start with the reader task, browse the category map, compare one nearby alternative, and keep each source in its own lane.
