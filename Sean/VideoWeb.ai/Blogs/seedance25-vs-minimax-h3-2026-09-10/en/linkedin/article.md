# A Short Video Creative Brief Template for Marketing-to-Creator Handoffs

A short-video request can arrive as a promising sentence: “We need something for the launch.” The creator still has to discover what the launch is meant to do, which person should recognize themselves in it, what must be true of the product, and what the viewer should do next. That discovery is creative work, but it should not be accidental.

I am a founder of VideoWeb AI.

This article offers a short video creative brief template for the moment before a creator begins. Its intended use is simple: make missing context visible while there is still time to talk about it. It is not a promise about any person’s work, production process, or tool. It does not replace a creator’s judgment.

## Start with the decision, not the asset

The useful question is rarely “What video should we make?” A better opening question is: “What should be clearer to the intended viewer after this short piece?” That question gives a marketing requester somewhere concrete to begin, while leaving the creator room to decide how the idea is expressed.

A brief is most useful when it records decisions that would otherwise remain in someone’s head. It is not a production calendar, a role map, or a record to be stored and routed through a system. It is one readable page that a requester can hand to a creator, then discuss together. If a detail is uncertain, the page should say so plainly instead of disguising a guess as a requirement.

The template below is deliberately small. It helps a marketing team explain the context around a requested short video without pretending that the page can settle every creative choice.

## The short video creative brief template

Use these headings as ordinary language, not as a form that needs to be completed perfectly. One or two sentences under each heading are usually enough to begin a useful handoff.

### The decision this video supports

State the business or audience moment in plain terms. For example: “Introduce our refillable travel bottle to people who want one item for a daily commute and a weekend walk.” This is more useful than “make a product video” because it names the situation around the request.

### The audience and their moment

Describe the person and the situation rather than a demographic label alone. What are they doing, noticing, comparing, or trying to avoid? A creator can then tell which details are essential to the situation and which are simply decoration.

### The one message to carry

Write one sentence that the viewer should be able to take away. A message might be “This bottle fits easily into a small day bag and is meant to be carried between stops.” The sentence need not become a line of dialogue. It gives the creator a meaning to protect while they choose scenes, pacing, and visual emphasis.

### The intended action

Name the next action the piece is meant to invite: explore a collection, visit a launch page, save an idea, or start a conversation. Keep it to one action. Multiple competing actions can make a request sound complete while leaving the creator to decide which one matters most.

### The non-negotiables and the open space

Separate what must be shown or avoided from what remains open to interpretation. A product color, a legal phrase supplied by the team, an accessibility consideration, or a location limitation belongs in the first group. Mood, framing, rhythm, and the order of images may belong in the second. This distinction is respectful: it identifies the boundaries without writing the creative work in advance.

### The references that carry context

Attach only the references that help someone understand the assignment: a current product image, a sample of approved brand language, a location reference, or a relevant source page. Add one short note beside each reference explaining why it is there. A folder of unlabelled inspiration can create another interpretation task; a small, explained selection keeps the context connected to the request.

### The question still on the table

End with the uncertainty that needs a conversation. It might be “Should this lead with the commuter moment or the refill detail?” Naming that question lets the creator respond with an informed point of view before work starts. A brief is allowed to have an unfinished edge.

## Make the distinction visible

Vague context is not always short. A long message can still leave the essential decision unstated. It may contain product adjectives, a deadline, and a collection of references without explaining the audience moment, the single message, or the intended action.

By contrast, a concise brief can be specific without becoming restrictive. It identifies the purpose of the request, what the viewer needs to understand, the boundaries around the work, and the unresolved question. The difference is not a claim that one arrangement will cause a particular outcome. It is a practical way to put the missing-context conversation on the table before creative work begins.

## Keep source links in their proper place

Sometimes a marketing requester wants to include product-page links while framing a short-video assignment. Put those links in the reference section and state why they are being shared. A creator may need brand language, a description, or a visual starting point from a source page; that is different from treating the page as an instruction that determines the creative decision.

For this reason, the brief should keep three things separate: the marketing decision, the source material, and the creator’s response. The requester owns the first. The sources provide context. The creator uses their own judgment to decide what the brief means on screen. That separation also makes it easier to spot a gap: perhaps the team supplied pages but never said what the audience should notice; perhaps it named an action but not the situation that makes the action meaningful.

## A five-minute handoff conversation

Before the creator leaves with the brief, try a short conversation around the page. The requester can read the audience moment and one-message sentence aloud. The creator can point to the constraint that needs clarification and restate the intended action in their own words. If the team disagrees, the disagreement belongs in the open-question line rather than in a silent assumption.

This is not a formal approval ritual. It is a small chance to discover whether two people are describing the same assignment. The creator may offer an alternative interpretation, identify a missing reference, or ask the requester to choose between two priorities. The page is doing its intended job when it makes that exchange easier to have; it does not settle the exchange by itself.

## Leave room for the creator’s craft

A marketing-to-creator handoff becomes less useful when it tries to prescribe every shot, sequence, or final expression. The team should be clear about the audience, message, intended action, and guardrails. The creator should retain room to decide how a viewer encounters those ideas. A good brief is a boundary around the request, not a substitute for creative authorship.

At the end, the creator’s reference pack can be modest: the one-page brief, a few explained references, and the question that still needs a decision. That is enough to begin a grounded conversation without turning the handoff into a task system or a record of later changes.

The most valuable part of a short video creative brief template is not the headings. It is the invitation to say what is known, what is constrained, and what still needs a human conversation. Start there, keep the page readable, and let the creator bring craft to the work that follows.
