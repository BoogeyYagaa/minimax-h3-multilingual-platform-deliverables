# A Short Video Launch Storyboard Template: From Opening Hook to Requested Action

A launch clip can begin as a small planning problem: decide what one short sequence is meant to make clear before anyone writes a generation request. This short video launch storyboard template is an editorial long-tail hypothesis, not a claim about search demand, popularity, or a formula for audience response. Its job is narrower: put a proposed opening, message, reveal or demonstration, and requested action in one reviewable order.

## Start with the decision the clip must make clear

Before choosing shots, name the decision behind the clip. Write one audience in plain language, one context in which they may see the clip, one message, and one requested action. A useful request is specific enough to review: “Show how the refill works, then ask viewers to read the product instructions” is easier to discuss than “make an exciting launch video.” The requested action is simply the next step the clip intends to ask for; it does not predict that anyone will take it.

Keep the scope to one clip and one decision. A launch may have a larger campaign, several audiences, and many channels, but placing all of that in a single short sequence makes the storyboard vague. This template is not a campaign plan, a generic creative brief, or a list of handoff fields for a team. It is a small decision map that can be reviewed before generation begins.

Use this one-sentence brief as the starting point:

> For **[audience]** in **[viewing context]**, this clip intends to make **[one message]** concrete and asks for **[requested action]**.

If that sentence cannot be completed without adding several messages or several actions, split the work into separate clips. The decision map should remain small enough that a reviewer can point to each beat and ask what it is there to communicate.

## Short video launch storyboard template: four intended beats

The four beats below are planning labels, not a proven sequence or a promise about a finished video. They give the planner a shared way to discuss order before prompts, source material, or generation settings enter the conversation.

### 1. Opening hook: name the first visible situation

Write the first visible situation, question, contrast, or object. The important choice is specificity. “A commuter notices a leaking bottle in a bag” gives a reviewer something concrete to picture; “start with energy” does not. The hook is an intended opening beat, not an assertion that it will stop scrolling or secure attention.

Add one boundary: what must not appear in this moment? That might be an unapproved color, a second product, an unsupported claim, or a scene that would shift the message. A storyboard is more useful when it records both the proposed visual and the constraint that keeps it in scope.

### 2. Single message: state the takeaway in ordinary language

The message is the one sentence the clip intends to make understandable. It should describe a product, offer, or idea without turning into a slogan pile. For example: “The cap can be opened with one hand” is a message. “Portable, premium, life-changing convenience” is a collection of loose claims.

Write the message beside the frame rather than trying to hide it inside imagery. That separation lets a reviewer ask whether the proposed visual is actually related to the sentence. It also prevents the storyboard from becoming a post-generation quality scorecard; at this stage, the task is to decide what the clip is meant to say, not to grade any output.

### 3. Reveal or demonstration: choose one fact to make visible

The third beat turns the message into a proposed visual moment. For a physical item, that could be one operation, one detail, or one before-and-after state that is factually supportable. For an announcement, it may be the moment a name, date, or simple use case is introduced. Describe what the viewer is intended to see and what stays outside the frame.

Avoid filling this beat with every feature. A single reveal is easier to review than a list of moving parts. It also keeps a planning distinction clear: “show the refill being inserted” is a storyboard instruction, while “prove the refill is superior” is a result claim the storyboard cannot establish.

### 4. Requested action: write the next step without predicting it

Finish with the action the launch intends to request, such as reading instructions, viewing a release page, joining a waitlist, or saving a date. State it as an ask, not as an outcome. “Invite viewers to read the launch notes” describes the planned beat. “Drive sign-ups” asserts a result that this template cannot promise.

The action should match the first sentence of the brief. If the message explains a feature, asking for a technical document may make sense. If the clip only introduces an event, asking the audience to study a long product comparison may not. This is a coherence check, not a conversion tactic.

## Turn the beats into a one-clip decision map

![Editorial illustration of one paper decision path through four unlabelled storyboard frames.](images/02-single-clip-decision-map-16x9.png)

*Editorial illustration: choose one clear sequence before adding generation details.*

Copy the following prompts into a planning document. Keep the answers brief enough to read in one sitting.

- **Clip decision:** What one idea should be clear when the sequence ends?
- **Audience and context:** Who is the clip for, and where is it expected to appear?
- **Opening hook:** What is the first visible situation? What must not appear?
- **Single message:** What one ordinary-language sentence does the clip intend to communicate?
- **Reveal or demonstration:** Which factual detail or operation is proposed for the third beat?
- **Requested action:** What next step does the clip ask of the viewer?
- **Boundaries:** Which claims, scenes, assets, or implied outcomes are outside this clip?

Here is a deliberately modest example. A maker introducing a refillable container might plan an opening shot of a bag and a loose cap, a message that the cap is designed for one-handed opening, a reveal of the refill being placed inside, and a final request to read the product instructions. The example does not state that the clip will gain attention, make sales, or establish product quality. It only gives the reviewer an ordered proposal to approve, revise, or split apart.

Notice what is absent: there is no scoring rubric for a generated demo, and there is no handoff checklist assigning tasks between a marketer and a creator. Those are useful but different tasks. This map ends once the intended sequence and its boundaries are clear.

## Keep generation notes separate from the storyboard

Once the decision map is settled, generation notes may describe source material, a visual style, motion, or framing. Keep them in a separate block. The storyboard records the intended narrative order; it does not claim that a tool accepts the document, follows it reliably, retains an asset, or produces a stronger launch clip.

These are provider descriptions, not official model-maker documentation, independent tests, or a basis for a model winner, output-quality, speed, price, availability, or performance claim.

That boundary matters in a planning document. A storyboard can name an intended visual and a source asset that a team wishes to use. It cannot establish that an asset will be preserved, that a sequence will be executed, or that a launch result will follow. Keep those questions for any later, separately designed production and review process.

## Review the plan before generating

![Editorial illustration of an unbranded desk prepared to review a four-frame launch storyboard.](images/03-storyboard-review-desk-16x9.png)

*Editorial illustration: review the intended sequence before generation.*

Read the map from left to right and ask a short set of decision questions:

- Does the opening show a specific situation rather than a mood word?
- Can the single message be written in one plain sentence?
- Is the reveal limited to one factual visual moment?
- Does the requested action follow from the message without claiming a response?
- Are unsupported claims, extra scenes, and unapproved assets named as boundaries?

If the answer to any of these is no, revise the map rather than adding more detail. The purpose of a short video launch storyboard template is not to make the plan look complete. It is to make one proposed sequence clear enough for a human decision before generation begins.

## Conclusion

A short video launch storyboard template can be a compact record of intent: one opening situation, one message, one reveal or demonstration, and one requested action. Used this way, it remains a pre-generation planning tool rather than a performance formula, a model test, a post-generation scorecard, or a team-handoff process. The next useful step is a human review of the four beats and their boundaries before any generation notes are added.
